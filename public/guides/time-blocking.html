Time blocking: plan the day in the unit you work in

A to-do list records intentions. A calendar records commitments. Time blocking is the practice of putting the work you intend to do onto the hours you actually have, then defending those hours from the things that would quietly replace them.

By Rajat Malik Published 17 November 2026 Updated 17 November 2026

The usual failure of a to-do list is not laziness. It is arithmetic. Ten items, each estimated at an hour, placed into a day that contains four usable hours is not a plan; it is a wish with bullet points. Time blocking forces the arithmetic into the open before the day starts, when you can still choose what to drop.

Why a list is not a plan

A list is good at one thing: getting the work out of your head so you stop rehearsing it. It is bad at capacity. It has no notion of a meeting that eats the morning, a context switch that costs twenty minutes, or the difference between shallow admin and work that needs a clear head. The result is a list that grows faster than the day and a permanent sense of being behind.

A calendar, even a rough one, carries different information: this hour is spoken for. That single constraint is what turns an intention into a decision. Cal Newport popularised the practice as "time-block planning"; the underlying idea is older and simpler than any book about it. Give the work a place, or it will not have one.

The method in five steps

1. List the outcomes, not the activities

"Work on the deck" is an activity. "Have slides 1–8 ready to send for review" is an outcome. An outcome can be finished, estimated and moved; an activity can absorb an entire afternoon and leave nothing to show.

2. Estimate in blocks, not hours

Choose a unit and stay with it: a 25-minute focus session, a 50-minute block, or a 90-minute deep block. Estimating in the same unit you work in removes a conversion step that people routinely get wrong. If a task needs "about an hour", it is either two sessions or one 50-minute block — decide which before it starts.

3. Put the blocks where the energy is

The best slot for hard work is the one where you are actually capable of it. For many people that is the first two hours of the working day; for some it is late at night. There is no universal answer, but there is a reliable observation: whatever slot you give to email first will not be available for deep work later.

4. Add buffers and breaks

Back-to-back blocks look efficient and behave badly. A ten-minute gap between meetings is where notes get written and the next context gets loaded. A lunch break that exists on the calendar is more likely to be taken than one that is merely implied. Put both in before you fill the rest.

5. Review the estimate, not your worth

At the end of the day, compare what you planned with what happened. The useful question is not "why was I slow?" but "which estimate was wrong, and by how much?" After a week, the pattern is usually obvious: a certain kind of task takes twice as long as you think, and a certain hour is reliably unusable. That is the information the plan was for.

A realistic day

This is an office day with two deep blocks and a meeting in the middle. It is deliberately not full.

Time Block What it is for
08:45–09:00 Plan Pick two outcomes, place them, check the real free hours.
09:00–10:30 Deep block 1 The hardest outcome. No email before it.
10:30–10:45 Break Stand up, water, look away from the screen.
10:45–11:30 Shallow block Email, messages, the small approvals that block other people.
11:30–12:30 Meeting Fixed. The buffer after it is not optional.
12:30–13:15 Lunch + buffer Notes from the meeting, then food. In that order is fine.
13:15–14:45 Deep block 2 The second outcome, or the first one continued if it needs it.
14:45–15:15 Slack Overrun, urgent requests, the task that lied about its size.
15:15–16:00 Admin Everything that is real work but does not need a clear head.
16:00–16:15 Shutdown Capture loose ends, choose tomorrow's first block, stop.

Two deep blocks, not five. The plan includes roughly ninety minutes that are not committed to anything, which is what keeps it alive when the 11:30 meeting runs long.

Rules that keep the plan honest

  • One outcome per deep block. A block with three outcomes is a list with a time attached.
  • Breaks are scheduled, not earned. If a block ends early, the break still happens; the next block starts on time.
  • Leave about 20 percent of the day unplanned. The overrun is not an exception; it is a normal part of a day with other people in it.
  • Protect the first block. It is the one most often traded away for email, and the one that makes the largest difference to the day.
  • Move, do not rebuild. When the plan breaks, move one block and drop the least important one. Rebuilding the whole day is procrastination with a calendar.

The plan is not a contract with reality. A day that matches its plan exactly usually means the plan was too easy. The measure is whether the important work got a protected slot and actually happened, not whether every line was honoured.

Common ways it goes wrong

Blocks that are too long

A four-hour "deep work" block is usually a two-hour block followed by two hours of pretending. Attention is not a tap. Ninety minutes is a serious block; two of those in a day is a good day.

No transition time

Moving from a meeting to a complex task takes longer than the calendar admits. The fifteen minutes after a meeting is not padding; it is where the previous context is written down and the next one is loaded.

Treating the calendar as a wish list

If the same block is moved every day, it is not a commitment. Either give it a smaller first version, give it a better slot, or admit it is not a priority this week.

Filling the breaks

A break filled with chores is a second task. The point is to change state: stand, walk, look at something distant, drink water. Fifteen minutes of that returns more than fifteen minutes of "just checking one thing".

Where DeepWork fits

DeepWork's Calendar page does the placement arithmetic from the tasks you already have. It takes the order you set in the Eisenhower matrix, lays the tasks out as focus blocks at the block length and long-break cadence your timer uses, and keeps the breaks reserved rather than filling them with work. A task that needs three blocks gets three. If you drop something into a specific slot by hand, that slot is fixed and the rest of the day flows around it.

The point is not that software must plan your day. A paper calendar and a pencil do the same job. The point is that the plan and the timer should agree on what a block is, so the day is not counted in one unit and planned in another.

Questions people actually ask

How long should a time block be?

For focused work, 60 to 90 minutes is a useful default, with a 10 to 15 minute break after it. If you use Pomodoro sessions, a block is usually three or four sessions. A block shorter than 30 minutes rarely survives the setup time for complex work.

What happens when a meeting overruns and the day's plan collapses?

Do not rebuild the whole day. Move the first unfinished block to the next free slot, drop the least important block, and keep the remaining deep block intact. A plan that can lose one item and still work is more useful than a perfect plan that is abandoned at 11am.

Is time blocking the same as a to-do list?

No. A to-do list records what you intend to do; a time-blocked calendar records when you intend to do it, against the hours you actually have. Most overloaded to-do lists are lists of outcomes with no capacity check attached.

How much of the day should be unplanned?

Keep roughly 20 percent of the working day free. That slack absorbs the overrun, the urgent request and the task that took longer than the estimate. A day planned to the minute has no room for reality and will be abandoned.
