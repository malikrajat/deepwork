A daily routine for two real blocks of deep work

The ambitious version of a deep work routine has five blocks, a cold plunge and a journal. The version that survives a Tuesday has two protected blocks and a way to stop. This is the second one.

By Rajat Malik Published 17 November 2026 Updated 17 November 2026

"Deep work" is a useful phrase because it names the thing that is actually scarce: uninterrupted attention on a cognitively demanding task. Cal Newport popularised the term in his 2016 book of the same name, but the experience is older than the label. Most people who do valuable work have some version of it, even if they never call it that.

The routine below is not a productivity system. It is a shape for a day that protects two blocks of that attention and, just as importantly, ends. Treat it as a starting point to adapt, not a standard to fail.

Start from energy, not hours

Two people can have the same four free hours and very different capacities for hard work. Attention is not a tap; it depends on sleep, food, stress and the time of day. The first decision in the routine is therefore not "what should I do?" but "when am I actually able to do it?"

For many people the best window is the first two hours after starting work, before email and meetings have claimed the day. For others it is late morning or late evening. There is no universal chronotype rule worth following over your own evidence. Track a week of sessions and look at when the good ones happened; that is better data than a quiz.

The day, in five parts

1. The ten-minute plan

Before the first block, choose the two outcomes that would make the day worthwhile. Not ten. Two. Put them on the calendar as blocks, check how many hours are actually free, and accept that the plan may lose one item. This is the step most people skip, and it is the step that prevents the day from being decided by whoever emailed first.

A useful check: if both outcomes are the kind of work that needs a clear head, they cannot both go in the post-lunch slot. Give one of them the morning.

2. Deep block one

Ninety minutes, or three or four Pomodoro sessions. One outcome. The phone out of reach, the chat client closed, the document open before the block starts. If a thought about something else arrives, write it on a scrap of paper and return. The block ends when the time ends, not when the work becomes difficult.

3. A real break

Fifteen minutes away from the screen. Not email, not a "quick" review, not the dishes. Stand up, walk, drink water, look at something further away than the monitor. The break is where the attention comes back; skipping it borrows from the second block.

4. Shallow work, deliberately

Email, messages, approvals, the meeting, the admin. This work is real and it has to happen. The point is that it happens in its own slot, after the deep work, rather than leaking into every hour. A single shallow block in the late morning and another in the afternoon is enough for most jobs.

5. The shutdown

Ten minutes before the end: capture every loose end in the task list, choose tomorrow's first block, and close the work. Newport calls the moment "shutdown complete" for a reason: the ritual is a signal, not a superstition. Unfinished work that stays in your head keeps running in the background all evening. Written down and scheduled, it can wait.

Two blocks is a good day. If the work is genuinely demanding, two ninety-minute blocks is around three hours of high-quality attention. That is a substantial day. Four blocks is exceptional, not the baseline.

When the day is fragmented

Some days have no ninety-minute window. Meetings arrive in pairs, a child is home, a release goes wrong. On those days, use the minimum viable version: one twenty-five-minute block on the most important outcome, protected properly. It is not the same as a full day, but it keeps the practice alive and usually produces something real.

The failure mode to avoid is the all-or-nothing response: "there is no point starting because I only have half an hour". Half an hour of undivided attention is more than most people get. Take it.

The weekly review

Once a week, spend twenty minutes looking at the record rather than the plan. Which blocks actually happened? Which outcomes took longer than estimated? Which day was reliably bad, and why? The answers are usually environmental — a standing meeting, a commute, a shared office — and therefore fixable.

The review is also where the next week's two outcomes get chosen. Keep it small. A weekly plan with three priorities is more likely to survive than one with twelve.

Questions people actually ask

What if I only have one free hour a day?

Then protect that hour and call it your one block. A daily hour of undivided attention is a serious practice; it will outproduce a grand plan that assumes four hours and delivers none. The routine scales down by dropping blocks, not by shrinking the protection.

Should deep work happen at the same time every day?

A fixed slot removes one decision and makes the habit easier, so it is a good default. Choose the time when you are genuinely capable of the work, not the time that looks disciplined. If your schedule changes weekly, fix the first available slot instead of the clock time.

Is a shutdown ritual necessary?

It is not mystical, but it is useful. Capturing loose ends, choosing tomorrow's first block and saying the day is over keeps unfinished work from following you into the evening. Without it, the brain keeps a background process open.

Give tomorrow two blocks

Plan the two outcomes, put them on the calendar, and let the timer keep the record. No account, no cloud, no subscription.
